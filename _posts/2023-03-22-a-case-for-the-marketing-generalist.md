Why the anti-specialist will thrive in a world with AI

*“You are the content you consume, even if it’s not consciously, your behavior and way of thinking will be affected.”*— Mark Manson

![](/assets/imported/a-case-for-the-marketing-generalist-01.png)

I admire people who devote their devote their entire lives to one pursuit: the writer who spends 12 hours a day at his laptop, the athlete who stays in the gym long after her teammates, the musician whose inseparable from his instrument.

I’ve always wanted to be this type of person. But after years of disappointment, flitting from one thing to the next, I’ve never been able to become this person. And maybe it’s not such a bad thing.

There’s a real push for specialization today: the playbook is to find a small niche, and ingratiate yourself into that niche until you know everything there is to know about it.

But in this blog, I’m going to make a case for the generalist — the marketer with a wide range of interests and skills. The anti-specialist.

**Serial Innovators**

It seems intuitive that the most successful people in a field are the ones who focus all their time and energy on that field. An idea that was popularized by Malcom Gladwell’s “10,000 hour rule.” But it turns out that this may not be the case -

Abbie Griffin, Dean of the Business School at the University of Utah, conducted an in-depth study on ‘Serial Innovators.’ [[here](https://www.amazon.com/Serial-Innovators-Individuals-Breakthrough-Innovations/dp/0804775974)] A term she used to classify people who are known for developing and commercializing numerous successful breakthrough innovations.

Here were the five common characteristics she notes:

#1: They had the ability to connect the dots across seemingly disparate pieces of information

#2: They have above-average levels of creativity

#3: They had a curiosity that extends across multiple domains

#4: They had an intuition based on deep expertise

#5: They had intrinsic motivation to solve customer problems for the greater good

This shows that even though the world seems to be shifting towards specialization, the most significant and disruptive innovations are typically made by folks who resist it — the anti-specialists. Although they have deep expertise around what they do, it’s not all they do — they also spend time exploring subjects outside their niche, forming a unique perspective that they bring to their work and use to generate innovative solutions.

David Epstein, author of Range and advocate for this generalist approach to one’s life, believes that being able to integrate knowledge from various fields is our competitive advantage in this world of automation and AI:

“The more constrained and repetitive a challenge, the more likely it will be automated, while great rewards will accrue to those who can take conceptual knowledge from one problem or domain and apply it in an entirely new one.”

As a marketer who isn’t necessarily “in love” with one specific aspect of my job, I took a lot of comfort in this sentiment. It encourages me to invest my time and energy in what interests me, rather than solely focusing on my chosen niche.

This doesn’t imply neglecting my niche or engaging in unproductive activities like binge-watching Netflix. Instead, it means being more receptive to following my curiosity, whether it leads me to explore subjects such as physiology, history, philosophy, or anything else.

**Collaborating with Your Experience**

In a podcast interview, Rick Rubin shared an insightful thought on collaboration and creativity:

“Everything we make is a collaboration because we’re not doing anything ourselves. Everything we do is based on the information that we take in, our experiences in life, what we learned in school, a conversation we had yesterday, all of the things that make us who we are, we bring into all of our projects. So we’re always in collaboration. It’s never our idea.” (find transcript [here](https://tim.blog/2023/01/16/rick-rubin-2-transcript/))

Another point in the ‘pros column’ for Generalists. It circles back to the idea that the most innovative, disruptive solutions will come from those with a diverse array of interests — those who ‘collaborate’ with different intersections of the world to develop a unique perspective.

I think this idea is important for marketers, whose job it is to devise creative solutions to marketing problems. Especially in a world of emerging AI, where creativity is our competitive advantage as ‘human marketers.’

Rick Rubin says we start our ‘collaboration’ during the “Seed Phase” of creativity. A Seed is any bit of creative inspiration that can be used in a future project. He explains that this phase is constant throughout the creative process, and one should always try to discover and take note of new seeds.

By being open to a diverse set of “Seeds” — hobbies, interests, and information — we cultivate a unique perspective that influences everything we do as marketers: from the way we frame problems to the solutions we come up with.

For the “Seed” phase, I like to keep notes of everything I come across that strikes me as important. Not necessarily things related to marketing, but from anywhere: books, T.V. shows, documentaries. And it’s not that I’m keeping this information for a specific cause or use, but because these are these seeds I want to ‘collaborate’ with.

**AI is Coming to Kill Your Mother**

The output of an AI model is only as good as the information that feeds it — the better the data set, the better the output. I think this is a helpful analogy for us human marketers too (I can’t believe I have to make this distinction, crazy world).

Except, the data set that influences our output is more than just the information we take in — it’s also the experiences we have. This is what gives human marketers such an advantage over AI.

Because when we take in information, we don’t just take it in — we feel it. The power of words lies not in the words themselves, but in the way we interpret them to actually mean something. And how that something affects us on a deep level.

This is made clear when we are captivated by a great piece of art. The value of the art is oftentimes inexplicable, and it resonates on levels far beyond the surface. And trying to dissect this to understand the objective principles of what makes it good is completely missing the point.

David Foster Wallace speaks of this phenomenon in the context of Franz Kafka, absurdist author, and literary genius. He compares the dissection of a Kafka piece through the “gears of your standard undergrad-course literary analysis” to “tearing the petals off and grinding them up and running the goo through a spectrometer to explain why a rose smells so pretty.”

This emotional resonance cannot be reduced to simple analytical breakdowns. As human marketers, our unique advantage lies in harnessing our emotions, experiences, and personal connections to surpass the limitations of AI. Although our creations may not parallel Kafka’s Metamorphosis, they will undoubtedly surpass the formulaic, best-practice-driven ads generated by AI.

So the more things we take in, the more things we feel, the largest our data set becomes. Our advantage comes when we use this data set of experiences to inform our marketing decisions.

If you’re a marketer who relies on “best practices” blogs and mimics industry leaders, you’re working with the same data set as AI. To create truly unique content that resonates with your audience, you must utilize your human experiences and capabilities instead of bringing yourself down to AI’s level.

We’re in the business of making connections. These connections are not made through concepts alone, but through the feelings those concepts evoke. Relying on best practices alone eliminates our human advantage.

Embrace your individuality and bring your diverse experiences — whether it’s that Netflix documentary you watched, or the trip to Vietnam you took in college, or that horribly embarrassing childhood memory — to your marketing.

Marketing needs *you*, not another AI operator.
