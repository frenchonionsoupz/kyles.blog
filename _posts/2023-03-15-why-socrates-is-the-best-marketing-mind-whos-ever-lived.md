#### “Socrates, to confess it frankly, is so close to me that almost always I fight a fight against him.” *Nietzsche (fragment, 1875)*

![](/assets/imported/why-socrates-is-the-best-marketing-mind-whos-ever-lived-01.png)

Socrates is the greatest Marketing guru who ever lived.

Not because of his portfolio. He actually has far fewer $100M exits than most of the marketing “influencers” you’ll see on LinkedIn & Twitter. But the method of inquiry attributed to Socrates, known as the Socratic Method, is an invaluable tool for marketers. It may even be the best marketing gimmick ever invented.

The Socratic Method is a centuries-old method of inquiry whose central theme is to question everything- asking even the most seemingly obvious questions. It’s been kept alive through millennia because of its ability to stimulate critical thinking and guide one toward greater understanding.

Why should marketers care? Well, great questions make a great marketer:

Great questions lead to a deep understanding of their target audience for more effective campaigns. Great questions clarify the goal of a campaign, bringing everything back to the company’s True North. Questions help us challenge assumptions, explore multiple viewpoints, and think more critically about the problems we’re trying to solve.

The goal of the Socratic Method isn’t to find truth; in fact, the Method discards the idea that there is one predetermined Truth that can be wrapped inside a box. Instead, it’s an exercise in *how* to think critically.

And while this may sound silly to the layperson — *who needs to practice how to think?* — a marketer knows very well how our biases can cloud decision-making. So think of the Socratic Method as a tool to clarify your thinking, so you can make your best marketing decisions.

In this article, I’m going to explain why I think studying the Socratic Method and the life of Socrates can be beneficial to marketers — both in the context of how it can help to battle harmful thoughts, and how it can promote beneficial patterns of thinking.

To Battle Harmful Habits

Harmful Habit #1: **“Best Practices Marketer”**

There’s a sort of marketer who I’ll refer to as the “best practices marketer.” The name explains it; this is the type of marketer who refers to a best practice when making any marketing decision; from small ones like when to send emails to big ones like which where to spend their ad budget.

This attitude is born out of risk aversion — not a bad thing in itself. But this fear of making wrong decisions leads some marketers to choose to copy the best practices of other brands instead of doing the critical thinking themselves.

This can become a problem for two reasons. 1 — For the company, you may be copying other brands that have different objectives and goals than yours — and so, what works for them, may not work for your company. 2 — For the individual, this habit of delegating your marketing decisions to the “best practices” creates less creative, less inspired, and less human marketers.

In a world with emerging AI, we don’t need copy-and-paste marketers. We need marketers who will come up with unique, creative solutions.

Harmful Habit #2: **The “I’m Not Biased” Bias**

Marketers are the first people to point out the bias in their audience. But the tricky thing about biases is that it’s a lot easier to spot them in others than in ourselves.

Ward Farnsworth, the author of *The Socratic Method: A Practitioner’s Handbook*, describes the Socratic Method as “an aid to better thinking and a remedy for bad habits of the mind.”

By questioning our beliefs and decisions, we can spot cognitive biases in ourselves and make marketing decisions with more clarity.

Harmful Habit #3: **Shiny Object Syndrome**

It’s hard *not* to fall into this habit as a marketer. It seems like our field is always clamoring about the next new thing and how it’ll change marketing forever — *and if you don’t get on board soon, you’re going to be left behind!!!*

But for the large majority of cases, the shiny new object is a distraction. Having a Socratic approach to marketing decisions will help you be more deliberate with the decisions you make — so that you’re never doing something just because “it’s the next big thing.” You do it because you’ve decided it fits in with your overall marketing goals.

We can never have absolute certainty about a campaign’s effectiveness before launching. But by testing our assumptions, we can at least ensure that our campaign is based on sound reasoning and evidence.

Benefits of the Socratic Method

Benefit #1: **Embracing Uncertainty**

Marketers have a bad habit of drawing broad conclusions based on limited evidence. We draw broad conclusions about best practices based on limited evidence from limited companies; we come up with “laws of human behavior” from limited psychological research.

It’s in our blood — we’ve been taught that data-driven decisions are the holy grail of marketing decisions. And so we use data to make our decisions seem more scientific than they truly are, but I think we’re often just fooling ourselves -

Economists John Kay and Mervyn King make a distinction between puzzles and mysteries in their book, Radical Uncertainty. Puzzles have well-defined rules and a single solution, while mysteries offer no clarity of definition or objectively correct solution — they are imbued with vagueness and indeterminacy.

Marketing problems are mysteries, not puzzles. There’s no one-size-fits-all solution, and what works today may not work tomorrow. By limiting ourselves to a single solution, we may be missing out on other solutions that would work better.

The Socratic approach to thinking helps marketers embrace uncertainty. By challenging our own beliefs and assumptions, we begin to realize that things we once thought were 100% true are not so certain after all. This approach makes it more difficult to accept anything as the “one, single truth,” and opens up a world of possibilities for solving marketing problems.

Benefit #2: **Fosters Creativity**

Using the Socratic Method can help marketers break free from the confines of best practices and open up a world of creativity. It encourages a type of thinking that many marketers neglect by deferring instead to the “tried and true.”

The Socratic Method involves two types of thinking that can aid creativity:

1 — **Finding similarities in different concepts**: By identifying similarities between seemingly unrelated concepts, marketers can develop creative solutions to problems that they may not have considered before. For example, applying a solution used in a completely different industry to solve a marketing challenge can lead to an innovative and effective solution.

2 — **Identifying differences in similar things**: By breaking down similar things into smaller parts and identifying critical distinctions, marketers can tailor their messaging and products to better meet the needs of specific consumer groups. For instance, a marketer may find that Men only convert for purchase at 11% and women at 23%. But by breaking these groups down even further, a marketer may find that men between 45–54 convert at 36% — by breaking these seemingly similar groups down further, a marketer will find that there are actually important distinctions to be made.

Overall, the Socratic Method encourages an open and curious mindset, challenging conventional thinking and seeking new and innovative solutions. All the components needed for breakthrough ideas and campaigns that differentiate a brand from the competition.

Benefit #3: **Asking Questions**

Approaching the world with a Socratic mode of inquiry will get you into the habit of asking questions about everything. And I mean *everything*.

Asking better questions as a marketer will make you a better marketer. Because good questions are the pickaxe you need to get to the marketing gold -

Great questions help you truly understand your audience, their underlying motivations, and their behavior. Great questions help you clarify the best campaign — why it will work, how it will aid your overarching marketing goals, and why this is valuable to your audience.

Questions can lead to innovation. “What would happen if we did the *opposite* of what we typically do?” or “What if we tested industry assumptions in this way?” It’s how you break away from the competition and start paving your own path.

—

The Socratic Method has been praised as a way to clarify one’s thoughts since the days of… well, Socrates.

I felt compelled to write this because I see a lot of surety in marketers today — I guess that’s because it’s difficult to become a popular marketing influencer while also being wishy-washy.

So I’m hoping that this piece can be a helpful guide to embracing uncertainty and opening up your mind to new possibilities as a marketer. Because with the rise of AI, marketers *need* to come up with creative solutions.

Those “best practice marketers” who merely copy industry norms and competitors won’t be able to survive. AI is much better at gathering data and replicating it. So we’re going to need to embrace the advantages we have over AI as marketers: our human-ness.

The ability to make connections, ask deep questions, and come up with novel ideas — *this* is what we need from marketers. And I hope that the Socratic Method can be a good way of helping you get there.
