<p>Garbage in, garbage out.</p><p>The information we consume has an incredible impact on who we are – our thoughts, our feelings, and our perception of reality. Yet it’s easy to overlook this fact when an algorithm dictates what content we see.</p><p>This is the main reason I stay off most social media.</p><p>It makes me feel hostage to its feed – content selected by an algorithm to maximize screen time and drive engagement (so companies can make more advertising $$$). And while that might be great for the platform, those aren’t my goals.</p><p>So what are my goals for the information I consume? To be honest, I hadn’t thought much about this until I started reflecting on it. I’d guess most people haven’t either.</p><p>But in an age where we’re being bombarded with information and opinions, I think it’s extremely important to be clear about our intentions.</p><p>So If I had to set intentions for <em>why</em> I’m consuming content, they’d look something like this:</p><ul><li><p>I want to be entertained.</p></li><li><p>I want to develop a better framework for dealing with the world.</p></li><li><p>I want to develop skills and certain domains.</p></li><li><p>I want to be informed about the state of the world and business.</p></li><li><p>I want to expand my understanding of the world and open myself up to new potential interests.</p></li><li><p>I want to develop empathy and understand different perspectives.</p></li></ul><p>When I look at this list, it’s clear how poorly the algorithm serves me. The content it prioritizes – bite-sized, dopamine-driven snippets – offers shallow engagement but rarely helps me meet any of these goals.</p><p>That’s why I’m attempting to be a better director of my “content diet”. I want to deliberately choose content that serves my goals rather than letting algorithms decide what consumes my attention.</p><h3>Entertainment is the gateway</h3><p>To be fair, my primary reason for consuming any sort of content is entertainment. And I think that’s true for most people – it’s why we’re drawn to social media in the first place.</p><p>But this is where I poke my first hole in social media: is it <em>really</em> entertaining?</p><p>Elizabeth Gilbert compares social media to a party drug that started off as really fun, and now everyone’s abusing it and no one’s getting high anymore. I love this analogy and resonate with it completely.</p><p>The initial reason I quit social media is because I was spending multiple hours per day scrolling. When I would finally close down Instagram or Twitter (thankfully, I quit before TikTok was able to infest my brain), I never had a feeling of <strong>“well that was fun!”</strong></p><p>Instead, the feeling was something like:</p><ul><li><p>Justified anger at some group</p></li><li><p>Dissatisfaction about my own life, compared to those I saw online.</p></li><li><p>Analysis paralysis about the "right" way to live, based on the countless options paraded in front of me.</p></li><li><p>Social disconnection.</p></li><li><p>Like I’d just wasted my time.</p></li></ul><p>When I’m in nature or having a deep conversation with friends, I leave feeling fulfilled. Not just during the activity, but it’s a lingering feeling.</p><p>And I noticed that no matter how much time I spend on social media, I can never find this feeling.</p><p>By the laws of nature, the more time I gave to social media, the fewer opportunities I had for experiences that truly fulfilled me.</p><p>So next time you spend a lot of time scrolling, close the app and ask yourself – “<em>was that really FUN</em>?”</p><h3>Beyond entertainment</h3><p>Then there are the other goals of content consumption.</p><p>If I had to make a big, blanket assumption (which I don’t <em>need</em> to, but will anyway), people are drawn to social media for the entertainment aspect – it’s when they’re considering their social media use or accused of “screen-time addiction” that these other motives arise.</p><ul><li><p>I want to develop a better framework for dealing with the world.</p></li><li><p>I want to develop skills.</p></li><li><p>I want to be informed about the state of the world and business.</p></li><li><p>I want to expand my understanding of the world and open myself up to new potential interests.</p></li><li><p>I want to develop empathy and understand different perspectives.</p></li></ul><p>The algorithm’s strength is in showing us ideas we wouldn’t have encountered otherwise. But it serves fragmented knowledge – great for sparking interest, not for building understanding.</p><p>I think social media is better used to spark curiosity about something, not as fuel to learn about that thing.</p><p>When something resonates, I take note and dive deeper through other content – podcasts, blogs, audiobooks, or conversations.</p><h3>A potential better approach</h3><p>It’s incredible the <em>pure quantity </em>of information that we can take in.</p><p>Some of it sticks. Most of it doesn’t.</p><p>That’s why I’ve developed a practice of reflecting on the content I’ve consumed – I do this twice per week by looking back at my content history (for me, it’s 99% podcasts) and writing down the takeaways that have stuck.</p><p>My goal in doing this isn’t to make the information more permanent (though this is a welcomed side effect). It’s to understand which points are most poignant to me – and that helps me consider which ideas I want to dive further into.</p><p>Because if my problem with the algorithm is that it directs my attention in ways I don’t want, then my solution is to understand the things <em>I do</em> want to pay attention to and direct it toward those.</p><p>Basically, I use this reflection as a compass.</p><p>In this process, I’m looking for two things:</p><p>1: <em>What</em> did I find most compelling?</p><p>This typically isn’t hard for me to find.</p><p>These pop out to me – but only when I do the work of reflecting. Because when I don’t, I simply get stuck in the pattern of consuming more and more content.</p><p>When something resonates with me, I immediately go into my podcast app and search for whatever topic I’m looking for. I’ll add a bunch of relevant episodes that seem interesting to my “Your Episodes” list (a Spotify thing).</p><p>If it <em>really</em> resonates with me upon further exploration, I’ll look into related audiobooks.</p><p>This becomes my listening for the upcoming week.</p><p>2: <em>Who</em> did I find most compelling?</p><p>We live in a world filled with snake oil salesmen, competing for our attention, engagement, and ultimately, our wallets.</p><p>It’s easy to get caught up in the whirlwind of convincing people pushing their agendas – not because <em>they</em> necessarily believe it, but because they want to convert you into a loyal follower of their brand or ideology.</p><p>That’s why I’m very careful about this part.</p><p>My goal is to find genuine people (disinterested in selling or shilling any particular idea) to follow, listen to their ideas, and take their recommendations.</p><p>The following is when my bullshit detector comes off:</p><p>– They treat their “solution” as being the only possible one.</p><p>– They speak with complete certainty.</p><p>– They dismiss all other perspectives and approaches as wrong.</p><p>– They're constantly trying to upsell you.</p><h3>In conclusion…</h3><p>While it may be easy to yearn for the past and have a pessimistic outlook on our modern, information-overloaded world, I do think there’s a lot of positive that comes from it.</p><p>We have unprecedented access to knowledge, perspectives, and opportunities – an endless library of inspiration at our fingertips. The challenge isn’t the abundance of information itself but how we navigate it. This is where intentionality comes into play.</p><p>It’s not about consuming less information (though that may be helpful too). It’s about being deliberate.</p>
