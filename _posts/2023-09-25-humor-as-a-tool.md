But I believe humor is as good a tool as any when navigating through this complicated, wonky world.&nbsp;</p><p>When thinking about many of these popular ‘protocols’ that are effective in improving mental well-being, there’s a common theme: they help remove us from our narrow-minded, self-absorbed perspectives. </p><p>As Arthur C. Brooks (<em>Harvard professor</em>, <em>bestselling author</em>) explains it - “what we need is a sense of the transcendent that makes us small.”</p><p>Let’s take meditation, a practice of quieting Not that I literally have that thought necessarily; I simply notice that the “me, me, me” chatter starts to slow down a bit.</p><p>Like my struggles and those debates I’ve been having in my head aren’t as important as I thought.</p><p>Our default perspective as humans is to focus inward: </p><p>this horrible traffic is happening <em>to me</em>; <br>this atrociously long line at the grocery is ruining <em>my day</em>; <br>so on and so forth.</p><p>I believe that humor has this same ability to remove us from our narrow-minded perspective, though only incompletely and temporarily. But it may be the easiest accessible tool we have.</p><p><strong>Humor &amp; the Holocaust</strong></p><div class="pullquote"><p><em>“Humor was another of the soul’s weapons in the fight for self-preservation.”</em> <strong>Viktor Frankl</strong></p></div><p>Viktor Frankl, an influential Austrian psychiatrist, spent three years in four concentration camps. His classic book <em>Man’s Search for Meaning</em> describes his experience through the holocaust and the profound impact on meaning in a person’s life.&nbsp;</p><p>These are his thoughts on humor:</p><blockquote><p>“<strong>It is well known that humor, more than anything else in the human make-up, can afford an aloofness and an ability to rise above any situation, even if only for a few seconds.</strong>”</p></blockquote><p>During the unimaginable suffering through his time in the camps, Frankl recognized how powerful a tool humor is. The following story shows how Frankl used it:</p><blockquote><p><em>I practically trained a friend of mine who worked next to me on the building site to develop a sense of humor. I suggested to him that we would promise each other to invent at least one amusing story daily, about some incident that could happen one day after our liberation.&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>He was a surgeon and had been an assistant on the staff of a large hospital. So I once tried to get him to smile by describing to him how he would be unable to lose the habits of camp life when he returned to his former work. On the building site (especially when the supervisor made his tour of inspection) the foreman encouraged us to work faster by shouting: “Action! Action!”&nbsp;</em></p><p><em>I told my friend, “One day you will be back in the operating room, performing a big abdominal operation. Suddenly an orderly will rush in announcing the arrival of the senior surgeon by shouting, ‘Action! Action!’</em></p></blockquote><p>I think this story highlights an uncommon perspective on humor. We tend to think about our sense of humor as something innate to us - some people have it, others don’t. And something I find funny may not be funny to you, that’s just how it works.</p><p>But in this story, Frankl treats his friend’s sense of humor as something to be developed as a tool for his survival. Later in the same book, Frankl refers to humor as “a trick learned while mastering the art of living.”</p><p>And it’s an idea that could be seen as offensive - one friend (Frankl), encouraging another to make light of the terrible circumstances they’ve been forced to endure. </p><p>So I wouldn’t tell others to laugh more when they’re going through shit. I imagine that’d only lead to pretty terrible outcomes. </p><p>But I can take this advice for myself and hope that when the time comes - when I’m inevitably faced with grief and hardship - I can laugh. About something, anything.</p><p>The entire perspective Frankl takes on humor changed my own. Thinking of humor as something to cultivate, not just for laughs, but for resilience. When further researching this topic (aka doing <em>one single </em>Google Search), I found this quote from Drs. Steven J. Wolin and Sybil Wolin’s book, <em>The Resilient Self</em>:</p><blockquote><p>“When we notice the humor in a situation, we are in an observant role. It takes a little bit of psychological distance in order to see the humor in ourselves and our circumstances.”</p></blockquote><p>To me, it makes sense that this is why humor can be so effective. The authors continue:</p><blockquote><p>“At the same time, humor isn’t exactly escapist; it doesn’t deny the awfulness of adversity.</p></blockquote><p>For example, people who have shared traumatic experience may have “inside jokes” about their suffering that are only appropriate to be told by and among those who have shared the adversity.&nbsp;</p><p>This isn’t negating the trauma. It’s not negating the victim’s experience. </p><p>It’s a way of staring the ugliness in the face, without allowing it to take your humanity. It’s an incredible testament to the human spirit - how we can always find a small piece of light, even through the darkest times.</p><p><strong>Coping with Life</strong></p><div class="pullquote"><p><em>“Our life has become so mechanized and electronified that one needs some kind of an elixir to make it bearable at all. And what is this elixir if not humor?”</em> <strong>László Feleki</strong></p></div><p>When viewing humor as a tool, the idea that some things just <em>can’t</em> be joked about makes less sense.&nbsp;</p><p>By not allowing ourselves to laugh at the tragic things, we remove a valuable tool in facing tragedy when it inevitably comes knocking.</p><p>I understand that the act of <em>not laughing</em> at something can be a powerful signal of one’s beliefs. Standing against a specific joke in a sort of rebellion puts an even bigger exclamation mark on your convictions. But I hope that these people, who are unwilling to laugh at the horrible in public, are still able to yield this tool behind closed doors.&nbsp;</p><p>And humor isn’t <em>just</em> a tool for dealing with tragedy. Here’s a a quote I found from US Army Ranger, Joe Asher about how humor got him through his incredibly difficult training:&nbsp;</p><blockquote><p>“If I can laugh once a day, every day I’m in Ranger School, I’ll make it through.”</p></blockquote><p>It gives us the ability to endure difficulty. And this isn’t just beneficial for you, it’s what we need as a society. Wade Davis, the famed Canadian anthropologist, says that “pessimism is an indulgence.” While this can seem like a harsh statement under many conditions, I think it’s ultimately a good pithy saying to live by.</p><p>Pessimism isn’t just negative for ourselves but also the world around us. Humanity’s greatness lies in our ability to develop innovative, previously unimaginable, solutions to hard problems. I believe that pessimism can block our innate propensity to devise these creative solutions.</p><p>And I’m not saying that a lack of a sense of humor necessarily leads to pessimism - but I do think that humor is a good way of avoiding that, even (or especially) in situations when brutal reality may seem to call for nothing <em>but</em> pessimism.&nbsp;</p><p>So don’t look on the bright side of things. Look on the funny side.</p><p>Another quote from Frankl to sum up:</p><blockquote><p><em>“Between stimulus and response, there is a space. In that space is our power to choose our response. In our response lies our growth and our freedom.” </em><strong>Viktor Frankl</strong></p></blockquote>
