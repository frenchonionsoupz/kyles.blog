*“The person who asks better questions gets better answers.”*— James Clear

![](/assets/imported/the-power-of-why-in-marketing-how-curiosity-makes-better-marketers-01.png)

When we’re young, we’re naturally curious and good at asking questions. Think of the toddler who persistently asks “why”, driving their parents to the brink of insanity.

In his Article “The Value of Asking Questions”, Ronald Vale from UCSF explains that young children ask questions that are “spawned by curiosity rather than a desire to impress.” This is the key to a great question. And while some people may have this built-in curiosity more than others, it’s something we can develop by asking more questions.

The most important part of Vale’s quote to me is the “desire to impress”. This urge to impress can prevent people from asking fundamental, seemingly obvious questions about a topic for fear of seeming “dumb” — yet these are often the most vital inquiries to make.

It’s normal for children to ask these extensive, oftentimes obnoxious, lines of questions. They’re not supposed to know these things. But as we grow older and become more conscious of our social standing and reputation, we can become embarrassed by not having all the answers. So we may adopt a false sense of certainty, pretending to have all the answers to avoid asking basic, seemingly embarrassing questions.

But some people don’t accept this false sense of security, and they stay curious. And when it comes to marketing, I’d argue that the best marketers are very curious people. In this field of marketing which is so uncertain, curiosity is our best tool for overriding humanity’s natural inclination to draw conclusions. In the curious mind, there are no final answers — just deeper questions.

In marketing, there’s never absolute certainty about what *will* or *will not* happen in the future. As much as many marketers may want it to, marketing will never be an exact science. As Peter Drucker says “the only thing we know about the future is that it will be different.” So while the past may inform our estimations about the future, they’ll never help us predict what will happen with exact certainty.

In marketing, every campaign is an experiment where the marketing team’s hypothesis is that “I think this will work.” Each time we run that campaign is an experiment — and while the results from that experiment may accurately predict future results from similar campaigns, they also may not.

Even the world’s best marketers don’t have this down to an exact science. The best marketers can’t tell you what will or will not work with any certainty. What they do better than other marketers is predict what will most likely work and what will most likely *not* work — an intuition that can only be developed through experience.

All this boils down to the reason I’m writing this blog: to explain why I think questioning is among the most vital skills for a marketer to have. In this field that rewards curiosity and punishes overconfidence, questions can be our best weapon.

Tim Ferriss has a quote about questions that I really like. He says “Questions are critically important because thinking is largely asking yourself questions and answering them.” This is something marketers need to keep in mind since our job often involes critical thinking and problem-solving.

In this blog, I’ll make the case for marketers to develop their questioning skills — I’ll discuss this in the context of questioning oneself rather than questioning others. And I’ll pose that this inner questioning has three main benefits to marketers: helping us think more clearly, think more creatively, and practice first principles thinking.

### **Think More Clearly**

A marketer’s most valuable tool is a three-letter word: *WHY*.

It can feel like an attack when someone throws this word our way. But in the privacy of our own minds, it becomes an invaluable tool for clear and critical thinking.

In marketing, it’s all too easy to lose sight of the *why* behind our actions: We establish overall goals in our marketing strategies and create action plans to achieve them, which include the tactics we’ll use and the metrics we’ll monitor to assess their effectiveness. And then the majority of a marketer’s time and effort is spent on these tactics — the hands-on, nitty-gritty work of implementation, integration, design, scheduling, etc. Even for marketing managers and CMOs, their primary role is often to monitor the metrics that measure the effectiveness of these tactics.

However, it’s crucial for marketers to remember that these metrics are merely stepping stones to achieving the end goal. The brand’s ultimate objective should always remain at the forefront of a marketer’s mind.

A great marketer keeps that overarching goal in mind and consistently refers back to the pivotal question: *WHY?* If the answer doesn’t align with your overall marketing objectives, or if it’s based on reasons like “it’s trendy” or “it worked for another company,” then it’s probably time to reevaluate.

Marketers tend to be bombarded by a constant influx of new trends and best practices. We see tactics prove successful for other companies, and so we want to mimic those tactics without truly considering how they’ll work for our specific business. But blindly adhering to trends and best practices can be counterproductive, wasteful, and even harmful to a brand — so we must rely on that all-powerful *WHY* to help bring us back to our overall goals.

Blindly following best practices also make us average marketers. Innovative solutions defy best practices, as Shane Snow explains: “If Apple had followed best practices, it would have released a six-button mouse instead of the zero-button Magic Mouse that doubled its market share overnight. Best practices don’t make you the best. They make you the average of everyone else who follows them.”

Questions help us avoid getting sucked in by the best practice vortex. They help clarify our intentions, so we can make sure that everything we do is pointing toward that true north — the company’s overall goals. Because even though marketers have a strong understanding of how bias influences others, I think we often forget about how it influences us.

Many of us fall for, what I call, the “I’m not biased” bias. It’s a common bias that infects those marketers with the smugness to believe they’re ‘above’ the biases they observe in others. When they study cognitive biases, they see these biases in others, but make no effort to see those biases in themselves.

Questions help us to test our assumptions, clarify our thinking, and acknowledge any biases we may hold so that they don’t affect our decision-making. The right questions help us align every decision we make with our company’s overall goals and ensure we’re making them for rational reasons.

### **Think More Creatively**

Creativity, as defined by the American Psychological Association, is the mental process leading to a new invention or solution to a problem.

I think the key phrase is “the mental process.” As much as it may seem like it at times, creative solutions don’t appear out of thin air — they usually take persistent mental effort. As Tim Ferriss suggests, this process of thinking comes down to asking and answering questions.

Creative thinking can be broken down into two essential types, both necessary to the creative process. Asking the right questions can facilitate both types of thinking.

The first is **divergent thinking**. The brainstorming phase of creativity — spitting out every single idea you can think of related to a topic: some good, some bad, some weird, and some truly horrible. But in this stage, all ideas are good ideas — it’s just about letting them out.

Great questions promote divergent thinking by encouraging multiple perspectives and considering alternative solutions. Questioning can promote divergent thinking. Some examples:

- If we had to market our product or service without using any traditional advertising methods, what innovative strategies could we employ?
- If we were to launch a viral marketing campaign, what kind of content or challenge could we create to engage our audience?
- Can you think of any unusual partnerships or collaborations that our target audience wouldn’t expect, but would appreciate?
- Can we incorporate gamification elements in our marketing strategies to make them more engaging and interactive?

This is the brainstorming phase and the more questions you ask, the better. Think about the weird, obscure, and complicated questions that your competitors probably aren’t considering.

The second type of thinking associated with creativity is **convergent thinking**. This is when you take all the loose ideas you had in the brainstorming, divergent thinking phase, and synthesize them into coherent ideas.

Questions help us refine our divergent thinking ideas. Thinking about the more specific, logistical questions that we avoid asking in the divergent phase such as -

- How does this align with the company’s overall marketing goals?
- Knowing the target audience, how would we expect them to respond to something like this?
- What would success look like if we executed this idea?

To sum up, creative questions evoke creative solutions.

### **Think More Fundamentally**

First principles are the basic foundational principles for literally anything. When you search the term on Google, you’ll see a lot of Elon Musk mumbo-jumbo and that’s because he re-popularized the term.

The polarizing billionaire credits first principles thinking for his successes with Tesla and now SpaceX. The process goes something like:

1 — Stripping away any assumptions you have about a problem.

2 — Breaking a problem down to its most basic elements.

3 — Rebuilding the solution from the ground up.

Musk took this strategy to the world’s largest testing ground: outer space. The SpaceX team used a first principles approach to completely rethink the traditional design of rockets, in favor or a reusable rocket system. SpaceX significantly reduced launch costs and achieved a record-breaking 180 successful launches in 2022.

First principles thinking is a powerful way to create non-linear results in a field. It’s why so many tech innovations are called “disruptions” of their respective industry. Because they break down the industry’s problems to their roots, they ignore all the assumptions that other industry leaders are playing by, and they create a completely new way to solve those problems.

Asking great questions can help remove us from our narrow perspective and challenge some of the underlying assumptions we may have unknowingly picked up about our industry. Take Uber for example, they started by asking questions about the taxi industry:

- *What problem are we trying to solve?* The process of matching people who need a taxi with a driver is too inefficient.
- *What is the problem at the root of that?* People need quicker, easier access to on-demand rides.
- *What are some of the assumptions that the entire industry is playing by, but aren’t critical to solving the root of the problem?* It’s necessary for the on-demand ride driver to have a Taxi certification.

By asking the right questions, Uber was able to see that this assumption the entire taxi industry had may not be true — so they questioned it. And of course, we all know how that turned out.

### **Final Thoughts**

If questions are a marketer’s best tool, then curiosity is the fuel that makes that tool run. I’m not sure curiosity is something you can fake either, at least not believably or for a long time. Authentic curiosity drives great marketers to ask the deep, insightful questions that lead to innovative solutions that other marketers are missing.

As well as making you a better marketer, curiosity also makes it a bit more enjoyable: Having a genuine desire to get into the minds of your target audience, see the world from a different perspective than your own, and offer potential solutions to marketing problems.

So I’ll try to stay curious. And maybe that’ll get me somewhere.
