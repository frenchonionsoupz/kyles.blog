I freelanced immediately out of college.

Then full-time for 9 months. Now, back to freelancing.

And I’m going to stick to freelancing. 23 years old, 2 years removed from a bachelor’s degree in marketing. It makes the most sense at this point in my career.

I know this isn’t the typical career trajectory. So I’m going to explain why I think this is the best path for me to take.

I can’t say that this is definitively the best way to do things. I can only speak to my experience.

Here are four reasons I think freelancing (self-employment) is a good move for a young marketer. 👇

**1. You get uncomfortable**

I was always shy. I had things to say, too much self-doubt to say them.

Freelancing forces you to speak your mind. You need to tell clients how you’re going to improve their business in a way that makes them actually believe you. Or you simply won’t have clients.

The term “imposter syndrome” gets thrown around. Freelancing forces you to get over that. You’re thrown into the deep end.

You need to be an expert at what you do when you freelance. And you need to communicate that expertise to the client, with confidence.

Why else would the client choose you — out of all the possible options? You need to give them a reason, or you’ll need to get used to selling yourself as “the cheap option.”

**2. You learn from trying**

When I began freelancing, I picked up any job I could get. Anything slightly related to the broad field we know as “marketing.”

A bunch of different industries. A bunch of different projects.

I ran Facebook ads for an EMS training course. An email marketing campaign for commercial UV disinfection. An SEO blog for Gatlinburg, Tennessee’s department of tourism (*seriously*).

I learned what I like to do and what I don’t. What I’m good at, what I’m not. The type of clients I like working with.

Through experience, I’ve narrowed down where I want to go with my career.

**3. You are responsible to YOU**

For the first time in my life, I was only responsible to myself.

Technically, I’ve always been responsible to myself. I just never actually cared.

I wouldn’t judge if I opted to play NBA 2K instead of studying for a test in high school. It was only if those bastards of parents found out that it really mattered.

In college, I had a bit more freedom. But I still had the structure of the classroom and the pressure of my parents who were funding the damn thing.

But when I started freelancing, there was no authority.

No boss, no teacher, no parents.

I needed to manage my own time and make sure I got things done. For nobody’s sake but my own.

I’ve learned to prioritize my work and allocate my resources for optimal productivity.

Not because there would be someone to “scold” me if I didn’t — but because I told myself I needed to.

**4. You get sh\*t done, unprompted**

When you start freelancing, there are lulls. Times when clients aren’t knocking on the door. And positivity inevitably dwindles.

This is when it comes time for business development.

But as an independent freelancer, *what does that even mean*…? That can mean so many things — from SEO blogs to YouTube videos to paid advertising to networking groups.

The scary part is that there is no right answer. There’s no one to tell you where to spend your time & focus — that’s something you’ll have to figure out on your own.

If you don’t make a choice and invest in at least one of these business development pipelines, you probably won’t make it as a freelancer. You definitely won’t grow.

So it’s constant testing things out, seeing what works, refining. Without anyone to direct you but you.

This is how freelancing cultivates a self-starter attitude.

Seth Godin describes a self-starter as someone who “turns nothing, or what certainly appears to every one else as nothing, into something.”

Cultivating this attitude will positively impact every company you work with. It’ll make you valuable way beyond the scope of your work.

“Has there ever been a project, an institution, or a community that hasn’t needed that?”
