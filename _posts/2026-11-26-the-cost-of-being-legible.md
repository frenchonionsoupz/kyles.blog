If I were considering an intro Taekwondo class, part of me would ask:

*Do I want to take this intro Taekwondo class*?

But then there’s another voice –

*Do I want to be the type of person who does Taekwondo*?  
(or in other words…)  
*Do I want to define myself in part as a Taekwondo-er?*

Most of this probably goes on subconsciously. Cus in my experience, just noticing this identity-level question immediately exposes its ridiculousness. *It’s an hour intro class, not that serious, dude.*

But this ~identity thing~ is present – and it’s the difference between just doing something & seeing myself as the type of person who does something. Watching myself instead of just living.

I could blame social media or our self-optimization culture. But I was incredibly shy through school, and when I reflect on it now, I think that’s largely because I was so conscious of how my peers labeled me.

Long before I had anything to sell or any reason to “optimize myself” for the market, I was already in the habit of experiencing my life partly through the eyes of other people and adjusting accordingly.

## But the world runs on labels<br><em>(or is that Dunkin?)</em>

To get through life, we label things: that’s a lamp, and that’s a dog. Turn the lamp’s knob, it will get brighter in here… do that to the dog, he’ll yelp and bite me.

It’s how we make sense of it all and simplify a complicated world, deciphering that thing from the rest of all things.

But the deepest part of me – that subject of my own experience (or *observer* of my experience) – resists being objectified. And when I am given a label, I feel misunderstood to at least some degree.

I experience myself from the inside, with all my complexity and contradictions. And all the shit that’s within my introspective awareness that I haven’t the words to describe – I’ve never even thought to search for them.

Other people experience me from the outside, and naturally reduce all that complexity into *something they can make sense of*.

Which is the key phrase, really – *something they can make sense of*. Recognizing this takes some of the sting out of it. People are just simplifying a complicated world so they can make sense of it.

In business, the usefulness of labels becomes pretty obvious.

People need to understand what you do in order to take out their wallets and hire you (*groundbreaking stuff, I know*).

This has been a struggle for me – I’ve spent a ridiculous amount of time trying to figure out how to define myself so that I can actually get clients and pay my friggan bills.

Cus as much as I’ve resisted being put in a box or putting myself in one, I recognize the value of it.

## <del><em>Friends</em></del> Labels with benefits

Labels can be helpful.

Don’t just go running, be a *runner*. Anoint yourself this label, wear the right clothes, download the right apps – and suddenly, you have a tribe. A community that thinks of you as one of them.

But the label comes with subtle demands you may not even notice.

Maybe you’re craving ice cream one night and ask yourself: *Is this something a runner would eat?* It may lead to a healthier decision, but you’re still consulting an externally constructed idea of who you are when deciding what to do.

And the less conscious we are of how these labels influence our decisions, the more likely we are to make choices we never would’ve made on our own.

Okay, maybe running is too mild an example for it to really hit home. Let’s try politics.

You agree with enough of a political party’s positions that you start identifying with it. Now people expect you to hold certain beliefs – even on issues you’ve never given much thought to.

And the relationship starts working in reverse: you adopted the label because of what you believed, and now the label is influencing what you believe.

**The more we defer to external forces and mute the voice inside of us screaming what it really wants… the less we’re going to be able to hear that voice**

Whether it’s what you want to eat, who you want to spend time with, or what you believe about the world – the labels you’ve adopted increasingly answer these questions on your behalf.

And I’ll repeat – the less conscious we are of how these labels influence our decisions, the more likely we are to make choices we never would’ve made on our own.

### And they may limit our life experience.

I believe we internalize the expectations that come with our labels more than we realize. And if we saw all the ways these labels limit us, we’d laugh at most of them.

Like - maybe you’ve decided you’re “not a dancer”… not even consciously, but it’s a belief that’s been sort of running in the background for your entire life.

Your Dad sort of instilled this in you, since you’re a man, and real men don’t dance after all. So, you’ve always avoided the dance floor or do a sorta half-joking lil dance when you get a drink or two in you.

But one night, you have a few more drinks than you predicted and you end up dancing, completely uninhibited, like a jackass. You see videos the next day and yeah… you were bad. It’s the most fun you’ve had in a long time.

Nobody was keeping you off the dance floor. You were.

Same with my Taekwondo example: nobody is asking me to turn an intro class into a question about who I am. I’m doing that on my own.

Their imagined voices (*“their” being some general blob out in the ether that represents people as a whole*) may ring in my head. But this pressure lives entirely within me.

We internalize these ideas about who we are, often without noticing what we’re giving up in the process.

## So that’s the struggle.

We live in what I call a [**Me-ritocracy**](https://kylejennings.substack.com/p/the-impacts-of-a-me-ritocracy): opportunities, deals and attention go to whoever is most visible and most willing to self-aggrandize.

Self-aggrandizement, meaning, attaching yourself to useful labels:

*-Call yourself a “thought leader” and get paid an outlandish amount of money to spew bullshit clichés on stage for 90 minutes.*

*-Brand yourself “the AI expert “ and become the person everyone forwards AI stuff to*

You can scroll LinkedIn and see the extremes of this yourself. I guess when millions of people compete for attention, nobody has time to actually know you and so labels end up doing more work.

Which leads me to feel stuck between two truths:

1. The world rewards people who label and sell themselves. If you want to build a business, spread an idea or help people, you probably have to play along a little.
2. Those same labels limit how you see yourself and who you let yourself become.

You might think you can play the game without being changed by it. But I think that’s underestimating its power. From Tim Ferriss:

***“Wear the mask long enough, you’ll become the mask.”***

At some point the strategy will stop being a strategy, and will simply become who you are.

So I guess that’s just the balance we have to wrestle with in our modern world. And like all things, there probably isn’t a “right” answer for how to approach it.

As the saying goes: **there are no solutions, only tradeoffs**.

I’m just trying to be cognizant of the tradeoffs.
